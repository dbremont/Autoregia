Autoregia

Autoregia is a forma for agency — a loose structure that shapes, guides, and forms agency without substituting for it, and that the agent sets down when not needed.

Human agents already possess the capacity to act, regulate themselves, manage resources and intentions, monitor their circumstances, and adapt their behavior without a formalized system. Autoregia does not seek to automate or replace these capacities. It provides an abstract organizational framework through which they can be externalized, structured, coordinated, and deliberately exercised.

In this sense, Autoregia functions as a forma for agency — a channel that shapes the flow without flowing, which the agent may enter, leave, or ignore — a set of structures and mechanisms that augment the agent’s ability to regulate itself, manage resources and intentions, observe its situation, execute operations, and adapt over time. Its purpose is to make agency more deliberate, coherent, resilient, and sustainable without displacing the agent as the locus of action and responsibility.

The Agent
A human agent is a stateful autonomous system that maintains its viability and pursues objectives through regulated interaction with its environment, recursively integrating perception, deliberation, intention, action, and feedback while continuously updating its state and adapting its behavior. Effective agency requires sufficient continuity of state, identity, and relational coherence across time for perception, deliberation, intention, action, and feedback to form a unified and persistent process.
Note — When multiple agents establish sufficiently coupled states, intentions, actions, and feedback processes, they can constitute joint agency: a temporally coherent process of coordinated agency that extends beyond the activity of any individual agent.
The agent · a stateful autonomous system maintaining internal representations of self and world
Agency Aspects & Problems
Agency can be understood as a set of interrelated functional problems that an autonomous agent must continuously resolve to maintain viability, pursue objectives, and adapt to changing circumstances. This spans from state and representation through information, deliberation, action, regulation, adaptation, and coordination.
The table below decomposes agency into these aspects and identifies the problem addressed by each.

Abstract Agency Framework

Abstract Agency Framework Formulation

The Abstract Agency Framework Formulation provides the tools, mechanisms, and organizational structure through which each agency function is realized in practice.
Human agents already possess the capacity to act, regulate themselves, manage resources and intentions, monitor their circumstances, and adapt their behavior without a formalized system; Autoregia does not automate or replace these capacities but provides the means to externalize, structure, and deliberately exercise them. Yet externalizing a capacity does not leave it unchanged — the structures, mechanisms, and tools of the forma alter what can be represented, remembered, coordinated, monitored, and acted upon, and introduce new forms of organization and feedback. In becoming part of the agent's mode of operation, the forma does not merely guide existing capacities: through its use they are extended, transformed, and reorganized, producing new patterns of representation, coordination, regulation, deliberation, and action — and with them, the agent and its agency themselves.

Scope & Limits

Scope and Limits

Autoregia's scope is to provide an abstract agency framework — a functional architecture that enables an agent to act coherently through time. It is an enabling infrastructure, not a goal in itself: it does not prescribe what the agent should value or pursue, only how the functions required to pursue anything can be organized into a sustained, self-correcting loop.
Two layers, two scopes. The abstract agency framework is not bound to any implementation — it applies to any agent that meets the definition. The concrete toolset built in the Autoregia project, by contrast, is fitted to a particular kind of agent: one that operates in a virtual environment — knowledge work mediated by software, where perception, decision, and execution all happen on a computer.
In Scope
Provided Description
Agency Architecture The functional decomposition of agency — perception, intention, decision, planning, execution, regulation, learning.
Organizing Principle The Control Loop that binds the functions into a coherent, self-correcting cycle sustained through time.
Concrete Systems & Tools The specific tools and structures that realize each agency function in practice — recording, reflection, planning, scheduling, task management — assembled into a coherent whole.
Reference Structure A mapping of those systems onto agency functions, usable to analyze and engineer personal agency.
Out of Scope (Limits)

Autoregia is a functional and architectural framework, not a theory of the agent's substrate. It specifies which functions every agent must implement and how they interrelate; it is silent on the empirical details of how any particular agent is built. It deliberately does not address:

Out of Scope Why
Psychology & Cognition The mechanisms of mind — emotion, motivation, desire, drives, habits as psychological phenomena.
Neuroscience & Biology The physical realization of perception, memory, and action in a biological substrate.
Ethics & Values What is worth pursuing. The framework holds values via policy; it does not supply them.
Theory of Agency Autoregia is a framework, not a theory of agency — it does not explain what agency fundamentally is. It presupposes only an operational definition of the agent (given in The Agent), not a metaphysical or scientific theory of agency.

Questions & Answers

Conceptual questions about the model, with working answers. Tap a question to expand.

A Model the world as a bounded region of state — entities, resources, and other agents — perceived through a time-ordered stream of discrete events. Events are the deltas; the Situation Model integrates them into a snapshot of current state. Four types constitute the stream: occurrence events (independent happenings — a message arrives, a deadline passes), outcome events (consequences of your own actions, i.e. the feedback signal), trigger events (time- or condition-based, e.g. alarms and scheduled commitments), and observational events (readings you actively take). So not the world as a bare sequence, but a state-bearing region perceived as a sequence of events.
A As the agent's own stateful substrate — the part of the world it regulates directly and observes with low latency. It holds memory (declarative, episodic, procedural), finite resources (time, energy, attention, money), beliefs and models, current commitments, and capabilities. In cybernetic terms these are the system's essential variables: the deliberative cycle reads them into the Situation Model, and the regulation layer corrects deviations (fatigue, overload, drift). It is bounded, depletable, and renewable — the reservoir the whole loop manages.
A A task is a bounded specification of work that represents an intended intervention (a commitment to transform the current state into a target state) by the agent to achieve, maintain, restore, investigate, create, or prevent a particular state of affairs. It is the fundamental operational unit of execution, defining what work is to be performed, under what conditions, and according to what constraints. Within the agent control loop, a task is materialized through Intent Management and Action Organization as the operational commitment to execution.

Every task originates from an underlying source — such as a goal, obligation, recurrent obligation, problem, opportunity, policy, plan, external request, or environmental event — that justifies its existence.
A A recurrent task is not a single task but a task generator: a template paired with a recurrence rule (interval, schedule, or trigger condition) that emits a discrete task instance each time the rule fires. Each emitted instance has its own lifecycle and completion, while the rule persists until terminated. Recurrence encodes routine and pre-commits repeated decisions, lowering deliberative load — it belongs jointly to System 1 (the instances) and System 2 coordination (the rhythm).
A Intent management is the process by which an agent continuously generates, evaluates, selects, commits to, and revises intentions that determine what it should pursue. It bridges perception and deliberation by transforming changes in the environment, internal state, and enduring commitments into an active set of intentions that guide planning and execution.

Intent generation is triggered whenever information suggests that attention or action may be warranted. The principal trigger classes are:

· Problematization — detection of deviations, unmet needs, failures, threats, or risks requiring intervention.
· Opportunity Discovery — recognition of favorable conditions that could produce value or advantage.
· Commitments & Obligations — goals, plans, policies, promises, deadlines, and recurring responsibilities requiring progress or maintenance.
· External Requests — commands, messages, delegated work, or other requests originating from external agents.
· Identity & Values — strategic objectives, principles, roles, and long-term aspirations that proactively generate intentions.
· Habits & Routines — recurring behaviors generated from persistent policies or recurrence rules rather than fresh deliberation.
· Curiosity & Exploration — uncertainty, knowledge gaps, prediction errors, or intrinsic motivation to acquire information or experiment.

These sources produce intent candidates, which are subsequently evaluated according to importance, urgency, expected value, feasibility, resource requirements, constraints, and strategic alignment. Conflicting candidates are prioritized and resolved, producing an Active Intent Set that directs planning and execution. Intentions remain active until completed, abandoned, superseded, or revised in response to new information or changing circumstances.
A A framework for agency is a coherent conceptual structure that identifies and organizes the essential functions an agent must perform to remain viable and effective — perception, intention formation, decision, planning, execution, regulation, and learning — together with the interfaces through which those functions interact and the organizing principle (the Control Loop) that steers them as a whole. It is neither an agent nor a specific tool; it is a reference architecture that decomposes the problem of agency into well-defined functional problems and specifies how they interrelate.

Four properties define it:

· Abstract and structural — it identifies what functions must exist and how they relate, independent of any particular implementation.
· Principle-organized — an organizing principle (the Control Loop) arranges the functions into a coherent whole rather than a flat list.
· Separation of concerns — it distinguishes perception from decision, decision from execution, execution from regulation, so each function can be examined and improved independently.
· Generative — it can be instantiated by concrete tools and practices, and reused to analyze, engineer, and improve any agent's capacity for self-regulated, goal-directed behavior.

Autoregia is such a framework: it provides a forma for the essential functions of agency — a channel that shapes the flow without flowing, which the agent may enter, leave, or ignore — which the Abstract Agency Framework Formulation then instantiates with concrete personal systems.
A Because commitment is the hinge of the loop: it is the step at which an intention stops being a candidate and starts consuming the agent's finite resources. Representing it explicitly matters for four reasons:

· Separation of generation from commitment — the agent generates many candidates but can only pursue a committed few. Without an explicit committed state, 'considered' and 'bound to' are indistinguishable, and deliberation cannot close.
· The Active Intent Set — commitment is what produces the authoritative set of what the agent is currently pursuing: the input to planning and the reference against which execution and feedback are compared.
· Feasibility & over-commitment — time, energy, and attention are finite. Commitment as a first-class state is what makes over-commitment, conflict, and drift inspectable and correctable rather than latent.
· Principled revision — an explicit state makes its transitions (pause, cancel, merge, reprioritize, replan) traceable, so intentions are revised deliberately instead of silently abandoned.

In the framework this is materialized as the Committed lifecycle state of the Intent Store and the Active Intent Set emitted by the Intent Scheduler — commitment is not an event but a maintained, revisable condition of the flow.
A An intentional structure is a structured representation of a desired state — of the self or of the world — that orients, motivates, and regulates the agent's cognition and action: the form the agent gives to what ought to be. It persists across moments, can be ranked and revised against others, and serves as the reference against which perception, deliberation, and feedback are interpreted. Its types differ not in kind but in scope, horizon, and binding force, cascading from identity to execution:

· Vision — the enduring, identity-level image of a desired future; maximally abstract, unbounded in horizon; it orients rather than directs. Ex: a life of disciplined, self-directed work sustained into old age.
· Mission — the vision translated into a domain of endeavor: what the agent is about, for whom, and to what end; it bounds the space of strategies. Ex: build cognitive infrastructure for engineering work.
· Strategy — a theory of advantage mediating mission and goals: where to concentrate resources, which paths to abandon. Ex: externalize everything; automate the repeatable; buy the commodity.
· Policy — a standing rule that pre-decides classes of future decisions; it permits, requires, or forbids — governance converted into habit. Ex: coherence over volume.
· Goal — a chosen end-state within the strategy's frame; directional and deliberate, but not yet a measurable commitment. Ex: ship v1 of the binnacle.
· Objective — a goal made operational: specific, measurable, time-bound; the unit against which progress is audited. Ex: twenty deep-work hours a week by end of quarter.
· Target — a quantified checkpoint on an objective; the finest measurable grain of intention. Ex: five PRs merged per week.
· Desired State — the atomic structure: a concrete condition of self or world to be brought about or maintained; the direct input to intent generation. Ex: inbox empty; all tests green.

The cascade Vision → Mission → Strategy → Policy → Goal → Objective → Target → Desired State runs from identity to execution: each level lends the next its justification, and the next lends the former its realization. In the framework these structures live in Policy (AGS) and feed Intent Management (AIAS).
A A work structure is a structured unit through which intentional structures are realized in action: a bounded, schedulable, and trackable parcel of organized effort, with its own scope, lifecycle, and place in a decomposition of what is to be done. Work structures answer not why — that is the question intentional structures answer — but what, in what form, and in what order. Their types differ in scale, duration, and degree of decomposition:

· Initiative — a named, sustained mobilization of resources toward strategic ends; it spans quarters or years and groups the projects that serve it. Ex: Autoregia itself.
· Program — a coordinated set of related projects managed as one because they share outcomes, timing, or resources; value is realized by the set, not the parts. Ex: the observation layer (PEOS + PKTS + PWTS).
· Project — a bounded body of work with a defined deliverable, start, and end; the primary unit of planning. Ex: migrate the binnacle records to CouchDB.
· Task — the fundamental operational unit of execution: a bounded specification of an intended intervention (see the earlier question on tasks). Ex: write the loop panel spec.
· Subtask — a decomposition of a task that retains its intent while being completable in one sitting. Ex: draft the gap formula section.
· Activity — the observed doing itself: executed, time-actual work, as distinct from the specification of work — what PWTS records. Ex: the forty minutes spent editing deploy.sh this morning.

The hierarchy Initiative → Program → Project → Task → Subtask descends from investment to action, and Activity closes the loop as the work as it actually happens. In the framework these are realized in AOOS's action constructs (Objective, Initiative, Project, Task, Routine, Commitment) and executed through ATE.
A Joint agency is a temporally coherent process of coordinated agency that arises when multiple agents establish sufficiently coupled states, intentions, actions, and feedback processes — a we that acts beyond what any individual agent does alone. The coupling is the essential ingredient: each agent's state updates and feedback become part of the others' control loops, so the resulting process acquires continuity and coherence of its own. It is therefore neither the sum of individual agencies nor their mere overlap. In the framework's aspects table it appears as the Joint Agency category, whose functional faces are Communication (Information Exchange), Social Coordination (Inter-Agent Coordination), and Delegation & Negotiation (Distributed Agency).
A Four properties are fundamental — they are what the definition of the human agent commits to:

· Statefulness — the agent maintains an internal state: models, resources, commitments, and capabilities that persist and inform action.
· Autonomy — it selects and executes actions from internal objectives and policies, not merely in response to external drive.
· Regulated interaction — perception, deliberation, intention, action, and feedback are recursively integrated through a control loop that corrects deviations.
· Temporal persistence — sufficient continuity and coherence across time make these stages one unified process rather than disconnected episodes.

The first three make an agent; the fourth makes it the same agent across time.
A Effectiveness is the joint product of the loop's stages, each with its own failure mode:

· Situational accuracy — the Situation Model approximates the relevant state of self and world well enough to act on.
· Deliberative quality — options are evaluated against values, constraints, risks, and costs, not impulse.
· Commitment discipline — the Active Intent Set is feasible; over-commitment is surfaced and corrected, not suffered.
· Execution capability — chosen actions can actually be carried out with the resources and capabilities at hand.
· Feedback closure — outcomes are observed and compared against intentions, so error is visible.
· Adaptation — the error changes future behavior, models, and policies.

None of these stages can be effective if the state they depend on does not persist (continuity) or contradicts itself (coherence) — which is why effective agency depends on both.
A Because the control loop's stages are separated in time. The perception that informs a decision, the intention formed in response, and the feedback that evaluates the outcome arrive at different moments; between them stand every other demand on the agent. Continuity is what lets the process span those gaps — the state that perceived is still available to the state that evaluates. Coherence is what keeps what spans the gaps mutually consistent — the evaluation is made against the same values and commitments that formed the intention. Without continuity the loop cannot close across time; without coherence it closes on contradictions — and a loop closed on contradictions still drives behavior, just not behavior the agent can recognize as its own.
A Continuity is the sufficient persistence and retrievability of state across time: the property that the agent confronting the present is the same state-generating process that confronted the past — its memory, identity, commitments, and working models remain available to it. It is the problem the Memory aspect addresses (State Continuity). Continuity is not immutability: state must change for the agent to learn and adapt. The requirement is sufficient persistence — enough for perception, deliberation, intention, action, and feedback to form one process, not so much that the past forecloses the future.
A Coherence is the mutual consistency of the agent's representations, commitments, and policies: beliefs that do not contradict one another, commitments that fit within capacity and do not violate declared values, actions that do not undermine the ends the agent says it pursues. It is the problem the Coherence aspect addresses (Internal Consistency), and in the framework the AGS checks it — over-allocation, constraint violation, and value drift. Where continuity is a relation across moments, coherence is a relation among parts at whatever moment they coexist.
A Continuity is the precondition; coherence is the constraint on what persists. Continuity without coherence preserves contradictions — the agent remembers everything and agrees with none of itself. Coherence without continuity cannot be maintained at all — a consistency check needs something that persists to check against. Together they state the minimal identity condition of a unified agent-process across time: what persists (continuity) and what may coexist without contradiction (coherence).
A Memory is the substrate of both. It realizes continuity: what persists and remains retrievable is remembered state. It practicizes coherence: commitments, policies, and models can be checked against each other only if they are remembered — an agent that forgets its commitments can neither violate nor honor them. The aspects table keeps the problems distinct — Memory → State Continuity, Coherence → Internal Consistency — on a shared substrate: semantic memory (GIS, PKS), episodic records (PBS), and the policy corpus (AGS) each carry a different part of what must persist and what must agree.
A Continuity is the axis on which adaptive change is regulated, and both extremes fail:

· Too little continuity → the agent becomes incoherent or reactive: each episode starts from scratch, accumulated meaning cannot accrue, and there is no stable identity for adaptation to serve.
· Too much continuity → the agent becomes rigid: past state over-determines the present, models stop revising, and adaptation becomes nominal.

Adaptation is therefore defined against continuity: change that updates behavior, strategies, and models while identity, commitments, and narrative remain intact. The requirement is not maximal persistence but sufficient persistence — continuity must absorb the lessons of feedback without dissolving the agent that learned them.
A Two forms, distinguished by the boundary within which consistency is required:

· Self-coherence across time — consistency within one agent's own states, representations, commitments, and policies as they persist through time: the agent does not contradict itself.
· Joint coherence across time — sustained mutual consistency among coupled agents: shared intentions do not conflict, interlocking commitments remain within each party's capacity, and feedback about the joint outcome reaches everyone whose intentions depend on it.

They are the same structural property — mutual non-contradiction sustained across time — drawn around different boundaries: the individual and the we.
A Joint coherence presupposes individual coherence but is not reducible to it. Each agent can be perfectly internally consistent while the pair remains incoherent — consistent-but-incompatible commitments, aligned beliefs about different worlds. Individual coherence is necessary: an incoherent agent cannot be a stable partner. It is not sufficient: joint coherence is a property of the coupled process, and it fails in two ways — from within, when any member's own state is inconsistent, and from without, when the coupling (communication, coordination, negotiation) is too weak to sustain mutual consistency.
A Separate, but coupled. Commitment is the general operation by which an intention stops being a candidate and becomes authoritative — resource-consuming, plan-directing, revision-tracking (see the earlier question on commitment in intent management). It exists wherever there is intention, individual or shared. Joint intentionality is one theater in which commitment operates, and it demands a specialized form: the joint commitment — conditional on mutuality (it binds only while the parties sustain it together), jointly addressable (each party can call the other to account), and rescindable by consent of both. Treating commitment as a separate concept lets one machinery — lifecycle states, feasibility checks, the Active Intent Set — serve both individual and joint cases, with jointness as a further constraint rather than a different mechanism.
A Both readings have standing. The strong reading treats coherence as cognition's organizing principle: dissonance reduction, narrative identity, and prediction-error minimization all describe a mind maintaining consistency across self and world. The modest reading treats it as one property among several — beside continuity, regulation, and adaptation. The framework takes the structural position: coherence is fundamental to agency as defined here — a definitional requirement of a unified, persistent process — while remaining agnostic on whether it is the organizing principle of cognition. That question concerns the substrate of mind, and the substrate is deliberately out of scope.

Appendix A · Viable System Model

The Reference Architecture

A Viable System Model (VSM) is a formal cybernetic model of the organizational structure required for an autonomous system to remain viable in a changing environment. It specifies the minimum set of interacting subsystems necessary to regulate operations, coordinate activities, adapt to environmental change, and preserve the identity and continuity of the system over time.
Where the agent control loop describes the temporal arc of agency — sense, decide, act, regulate — the Viable System Model describes its structural complement: the minimum set of interacting subsystems any viable system must implement to keep that loop running. Autoregia uses the VSM as its reference architecture, mapping every personal system to one of these functions.
The Five Systems
System Name Function
System 1 Operational Units The primary units that perform the agent's basic operations. Each unit is semi-autonomous and responsible for delivering specific outputs or services.
System 2 Coordination Ensures the harmonious operation of System 1 units by providing coordination and conflict resolution, managing the interactions and synergies between operational units.
System 3 Control Provides resource allocation, monitoring, and control over the operational units; ensures the system is functioning efficiently and effectively.
System 3* Audit A subset of System 3 focused on monitoring and auditing the operational units to ensure compliance and performance standards.
System 4 Intelligence Focuses on the external environment and future planning; adapts the system to environmental change, drives innovation and strategic development.
System 5 Policy Sets the system's overall goals, values, and direction; balances internal and external focus and maintains identity and cohesion.

ACSMS Agent Capability Self Management System.
Active Intent Set The authoritative set of intentions the agent is currently pursuing — the output of commitment, the input to planning, and the reference against which execution and feedback are compared.
AGS Agency Grounding System.
Agent A stateful autonomous system that maintains its viability and pursues objectives through regulated interaction with its environment, using feedback to update its internal state, formulate intentions, select and execute actions, and adapt its behavior.
Agency The set of interrelated functional problems an autonomous agent must continuously resolve to maintain viability, pursue objectives, and adapt to changing circumstances — from state and representation through information, deliberation, action, regulation, adaptation, and coordination.
AIAS Agent Intent Aid System.
AOOS Agent Operation Organization System.
ATE Agent Toolbox Ecosystem.
AWES Automated Work Execution System.
Commitment The hinge of the loop: the step at which an intention stops being a candidate and starts consuming the agent's finite resources. Not an event but a maintained, revisable condition (pause, cancel, merge, reprioritize, replan).
Control Loop The organizing principle of the framework: the continuous cycle of deliberation, action, feedback, regulation, and adaptation — the temporal arc of agency (sense, decide, act, regulate).
Environment (World) A bounded region of state — entities, resources, and other agents — perceived through a time-ordered stream of discrete events. Not the world as a bare sequence, but a state-bearing region perceived as a sequence.
Forma What the framework is: a channel that shapes the flow without flowing, which the agent may enter, leave, or ignore. It externalizes, structures, and supports the exercise of agency — making it more deliberate, coherent, resilient, and sustainable — without displacing the agent as the locus of action and responsibility.
Event A delta in the world stream. Four types constitute it: occurrence (independent happenings), outcome (consequences of the agent's own actions — the feedback signal), trigger (time- or condition-based, e.g. alarms and scheduled commitments), and observational (readings the agent actively takes).
GIAL General Integration Abstraction Layer.
GIS General Index System.
GWOB General World and Self Observation System.
Intent A candidate direction of pursuit, generated by intent management and evaluated against importance, urgency, expected value, feasibility, resource requirements, constraints, and strategic alignment.
Internal environment The agent's own stateful substrate, the part of the world it regulates directly and observes with low latency: memory (declarative, episodic, procedural), finite resources (time, energy, attention, money), beliefs and models, current commitments, and capabilities — bounded, depletable, and renewable.
Observation A reading the agent actively takes; the event type realized by the external-observation systems (PEOS).
PBS Personal Binnacle System.
PEOS Personal External Observation System.
PKS Personal Knowledge System.
PKTS Personal Keyword Tracking System.
Policy The normative layer: principles, values, constraints, and enduring commitments that determine what the agent may, should, or must pursue. The framework holds values via policy; it does not supply them.
PRAS Personal Reflection & Adaptation System.
PVSM Personal Viable System Model.
PWTS Personal Workstation Tracking System.
Recurrent task A task generator: a template paired with a recurrence rule (interval, schedule, or trigger condition) that emits a discrete task instance each time the rule fires; instances have their own lifecycle while the rule persists until terminated.
SARL Sistema Asistencia de Revisión Lingüística.
Situation Model The integration of the event stream into a snapshot of current state — the agent's best current estimate of the relevant state of itself and its environment.
System 1 Operational Units — the primary units that perform the agent's basic operations. Each unit is semi-autonomous and responsible for delivering specific outputs or services.
System 2 Coordination — ensures the harmonious operation of System 1 units by providing coordination and conflict resolution, managing the interactions and synergies between operational units.
System 3 Control — provides resource allocation, monitoring, and control over the operational units; ensures the system is functioning efficiently and effectively.
System 3* Audit — a subset of System 3 focused on monitoring and auditing the operational units to ensure compliance and performance standards.
System 4 Intelligence — focuses on the external environment and future planning; adapts the system to environmental change, drives innovation and strategic development.
System 5 Policy — sets the system's overall goals, values, and direction; balances internal and external focus and maintains identity and cohesion.
Task A bounded specification of work: an intended intervention — a commitment to transform the current state into a target state — to achieve, maintain, restore, investigate, create, or prevent a state of affairs; the fundamental operational unit of execution.
Viability The condition of remaining coherent, functional, and sustained over time in a changing environment; the property the whole loop exists to preserve.
